<b>Key takeaways</b>

- You can make a complete batch of ID cards on an Android phone: **plan the fields, collect the list, take photos, apply a template, check, then export a print-ready PDF**.
- Set the card to **CR80 (85.6 × 54 mm)** unless you have a reason not to, and export at **300 DPI** so text and photos print sharp.
- For more than a handful of people, **type the list in a spreadsheet and import it** (Excel or CSV) instead of typing names on the phone keyboard.
- Print the A4 PDF at **100% / Actual size** from the phone or at a print shop: **10 landscape or 9 portrait cards per sheet**.
- Check every card for a missing photo, misspelt name and wrong expiry date **before** you print, not after you laminate.

Most people who search for how to make ID cards on mobile are not designers. They are a head teacher, an HR officer or an event volunteer with a list of names, a deadline and only a phone. The good news is that a phone is now enough for the whole job, from the first photo to a stack of printed cards.

This guide walks through the full workflow in the order you actually need it, with the sizes, file settings and checks that stop a batch from going wrong. It is the overview page for our blog: each step links to a deeper guide where one exists.

## What you need before you start

You do not need a computer, a card printer or design software. You need five things, and gathering them first saves hours later.

| What | Why you need it | Tip |
|---|---|---|
| An Android phone with a decent camera | Takes the photos and runs the ID card maker app | Any phone from the last few years has far more resolution than a card photo needs |
| The list of people | Names, ID numbers, department or class, and any other fields | Get it as a spreadsheet from HR or the school office if one already exists |
| Your logo | Goes in the card header | A PNG with a transparent background looks best on coloured headers |

## Step 1: Plan the card type, size and fields

Decide three things before you open any app, because changing them later means rechecking every card.

**Card type.** Staff, student, visitor, event and membership cards carry different information. A student card needs class and guardian phone; a visitor pass needs a date and a host name; an event badge needs a big first name.

**Size and orientation.** The standard is **CR80, 85.6 × 54 mm**, the same as a bank card, and it fits every standard holder. Portrait suits lanyards; landscape suits wallets and clip holders. Event badges are often larger (4 × 3 in, 3R, 4R or A6). Our [ID card size chart](/blog/id-card-size-cr80-mm-inches-pixels/) has every size in mm, inches and pixels.

**Fields.** Keep the front to what someone needs to identify the holder at a glance, and move everything else to the back. For staff cards in particular, our [employee ID card design guide](/blog/employee-id-card-design-what-to-include/) lists every field, what never to print and the font sizes to use.

| Card type | Front (essential) | Back (useful) |
|---|---|---|
| Employee ID | Photo, name, designation, department, ID number, logo | Blood group, emergency phone, validity, "if found" text, signature |
| Student ID (school or madrasa) | Photo, name, class, section, roll, institution name | Guardian name and phone, blood group, session, address of the school |
| Event pass | First name (large), surname, organisation, attendee type | Schedule, Wi-Fi, organiser phone, QR code |
| Visitor pass | "VISITOR", pass number, date, host or department | Return instructions, rules for the site |
| Membership card | Photo, name, member number, valid until | Terms, renewal contact |

> **Tip:** Leave home addresses, personal mobile numbers of children and national ID numbers off the card. If a card is lost, whoever finds it should be able to return it, not learn where the holder lives.

## Step 2: Set up your organisation once

Every card in the batch shares the same header, logo and signature, so set these up first.

1. **Enter the organisation's name, type and address** exactly as they should appear. Check the official spelling with the office; a typo here is on every single card.
2. **Add the logo.** Use the largest, cleanest version you have. A logo copied from a WhatsApp message is usually compressed and prints blurry.
3. **Capture the signature.** Ask the signatory to sign on plain white paper with a dark pen, then photograph it flat in good light. A good ID card maker app removes the white paper so the signature sits cleanly on any background.
4. **Add departments, designations or classes** you will reuse, so you pick them from a list instead of typing "Acounts" on card 37.

## Step 3: Collect the data (and import it, don't type it)

For five or ten people, typing on the phone is fine. For a class of 60 or a factory floor of 400, typing names on a phone keyboard is where most errors come from. Use a spreadsheet instead.

**Set up the sheet like this:**

- One row per person, one header row at the top, no merged cells and no blank rows in the middle.
- One column per field: Name, ID number, Designation or Class, Department or Section, Phone, Blood group, Valid until.
- Write ID numbers as text so leading zeros survive (in Excel, type an apostrophe before `0045`, or format the column as Text).
- Use one date format for the whole column, for example `31-12-2027`.
- If names are in Bangla, save as **CSV UTF-8** from Excel (File → Save As → "CSV UTF-8 (Comma delimited)"). A plain CSV can turn Bangla text into question marks.

You can build the sheet in Excel or Google Sheets on a computer, or in the Sheets app on the same phone, then import the file. A reliable trick: add one person by hand in the app, **export** the list, and use that exported file as your template. The column order then matches what the app expects.

> **Tip:** Sort the sheet by class or department before importing. Cards then print in the same order you will hand them out, which makes distribution day much faster.

## Step 4: Take photos that print well

The photo makes or breaks an ID card, and a phone can take a better ID photo than most people expect, if you control the light and background. (For the full setup, exact crop sizes and a retake checklist, see our guide on [how to take an ID card photo with your phone](/blog/how-to-take-id-card-photo-with-phone/).)

- **Background:** a plain, light wall or a sheet of white chart paper taped behind the chair. Avoid patterned curtains and windows behind the person.
- **Light:** face a window or a bright light, so the face is evenly lit. Light from behind turns the face dark.
- **Framing:** head and top of the shoulders, face looking straight at the camera, with a little space above the head. Crop to the card's photo box ratio, roughly 3:4 or 4:5.
- **Distance:** stand about 1–1.5 m away and zoom slightly rather than holding the phone close, which distorts noses and foreheads.
- **Resolution:** the photo box on a CR80 card is about 22 × 28 mm, which is only about 260 × 331 pixels at 300 DPI. Any phone camera covers that many times over, so blur comes from shake or bad light, not from the camera.

For a whole school or office, run a **photo day**: one person calls names from the list, one person takes photos against the same background, and you name or tag each photo with the person's ID number as you go. If people send their own photos instead, you can add them to the app in bulk afterwards and crop each one to match.

## Step 5: Choose a template and adjust the design

With clean data and photos, design takes minutes. Pick a template that fits the card type, then adjust:

- **Colours:** use your organisation's main colour for the header band or side stripe, and keep text dark on light or white on a strong colour. Pale yellow or light grey text on white disappears when printed.
- **Hierarchy:** the name should be the largest text after the organisation name, around 9–12 pt. Other details can be 6.5–8 pt, but do not go below 6 pt.
- **Safe zone:** keep text, photo and logo at least 3 mm inside the card edge. Cutting is never perfect and corners are rounded.
- **Long names:** check the longest name in your list on the sample card. If it wraps badly, reduce the name size slightly for everyone rather than squeezing one card.
- **Back side:** add the back now, not as an afterthought: emergency contact, validity, return text and signature.

Then make **one sample card** with a real person's data, export it, and get it approved by whoever signs off.

## Step 6: Check every card before printing

This step saves paper, ink and lamination pouches. Work through this checklist on the phone screen:

- Every person has a photo, and no photo is sideways, dark or someone else's.
- Names are spelt as the person writes them (check against the office register, not memory).
- ID numbers are unique and none lost their leading zeros.
- Department, class and designation use the same wording on every card.
- Validity or expiry dates are correct, and no card is already expiring within the next month.
- Blood group and emergency phone, if printed, were confirmed by the person or guardian.
- Nothing touches the card edge, and the longest name fits.
- The back side is set up and matches the front orientation.
- Logo and signature are sharp at full zoom.

A dashboard that flags people with a missing photo or incomplete details, and cards expiring soon, turns this from a one-by-one scroll into a two-minute check. CardCraft has this dashboard built in.

## Step 7: Export and print from your phone

Once every card passes the check, export the batch.

1. **Export a print-ready PDF at 300 DPI**, laid out on A4 sheets with cut marks. Use PNG images instead if you need individual card files, for example to send to a card-printer operator or to share digitally.
2. **Decide where to print.** From the phone, use Android's print option with a Wi-Fi printer (Android supports this through its built-in print service or the free [Mopria Print Service](https://mopria.org/how-to-print)). Or copy the PDF to a pen drive or send it to a print shop.
3. **Print at 100% / Actual size**, never "Fit to page", which shrinks every card by a few percent.
4. **Print one test sheet on plain paper**, measure a card with a ruler (it should be 85.5–86 mm wide), then print the real batch on card stock or PVC sheets.
5. **Print the backs** on the reverse of the same sheets, cut along the marks and laminate.

Our guide on [how to print ID cards on A4 paper](/blog/how-to-print-id-cards-on-a4-paper/) covers layouts, paper weights, double-sided alignment and cutting in detail.

## Worked example: 60 student cards from one phone

Here is what the numbers look like for a typical school class, so you can plan paper and time.

| Item | Portrait cards (3 × 3 on A4) | Landscape cards (2 × 5 on A4) |
|---|---|---|
| Cards per sheet | 9 | 10 |
| Sheets for 60 cards (front) | 7 (the last sheet has 6 cards) | 6 |
| Sheets with backs | 14 sides on 7 sheets | 12 sides on 6 sheets |
| Test sheets on plain paper | 1–2 | 1–2 |
| Lamination pouches | 63 (60 + about 5% spare) | 63 (60 + about 5% spare) |

The landscape layout saves one sheet per 60 cards, but most schools prefer portrait cards on lanyards. Choose the orientation that suits how the card is worn, not the one that saves a sheet of paper.

A realistic plan is one day for data and photos, an hour for design and checking, and an afternoon for printing, cutting and laminating.

## Common mistakes when making ID cards on a phone

- **Typing 400 names by hand.** Import a spreadsheet; it is faster and easier to proofread.
- **No sample approval.** Reprinting a whole batch because of a logo size or a wrong title costs far more than waiting a day for approval.
- **Printing with "Fit to page".** Cards come out slightly small and look loose in holders.
- **Forgetting backups.** Your card data lives on the phone. Save a backup file before you change phones or reset the device.

## Frequently asked questions

### How can I make an ID card on my mobile phone?

Install an ID card maker app, set up your organisation's name, logo and signature, add people by typing or importing a spreadsheet, and take or add their photos. Choose a template, check each card, then export a 300 DPI PDF and print it at 100% scale on A4 paper or card stock.

### Which is the best ID card maker app for Android?

Look for an app that uses real card sizes such as CR80, exports at 300 DPI, prints A4 sheets with cut marks, supports front and back, and imports Excel or CSV lists if you have more than a few people. Offline use and Bangla support matter in Bangladesh. CardCraft is built around exactly that workflow, and it is free for up to 10 people.

### Can I make an ID card with a photo on my phone?

Yes. Take the photo in the app or pick one from your gallery, crop it to the photo box and place it on the card. Use a plain light background and face a window or lamp so the face is evenly lit; that matters more than the camera.

### Can I print an ID card directly from my phone?

Yes, if your printer is on the same Wi-Fi network. Android can print to most modern printers through its built-in print service or the Mopria Print Service app. Choose 100% or "Actual size" and print a test page first. Otherwise, take the PDF to a print shop.

### How many ID cards can I print on one A4 sheet?

Ten landscape CR80 cards (2 × 5) or nine portrait cards (3 × 3), with small gaps for cutting. Larger badges fit fewer: four A6 passes per A4 sheet, for example.

### Can I make a national ID card or other government ID with an app?

No. ID card maker apps like CardCraft are for your own organisation's cards: staff, students, members, visitors and events. National ID, passports, driving licences and other government documents can only be issued by the government, and imitating them is illegal.
