<b>Key takeaways</b>

- A good ID card photo needs **a plain light background, soft light from the front, the phone at eye level and the rear camera about 1.5 m away** with a little zoom. The camera itself is almost never the problem.
- Frame **head and top of the shoulders**, face straight on, eyes open, neutral expression, and leave a small gap above the hair. Crop to the card's photo box, usually **3:4 or 4:5**.
- On a standard CR80 card the photo box is about **22 × 28 mm to 26 × 32 mm**, which is only **260 × 331 to 307 × 378 pixels at 300 DPI**. Any phone from the last few years has many times that.
- For a whole school or office, run a **photo day**: one fixed spot, one background, one light setup, and every photo labelled with the person's ID number as it is taken.
- The most common rejects are **a shadow on the wall, a dark face from a window behind the person, a tilted head and wide-angle selfies** that make the nose look big. All four are fixed by the setup, not by editing.
</div>

Most bad ID card photos are not taken with a bad camera. They are taken in a corridor with the light behind the person, from too close, against a notice board. Knowing how to take an ID card photo properly takes five minutes to learn and saves you reprinting a stack of cards.

This guide covers the setup (background, light, distance and height), the framing rules, exact crop sizes in millimetres and pixels for common card layouts, a checklist to approve or reject each shot, and a plan for photographing a whole school, factory or office in one day.

## What makes a good ID card photo?

An ID card photo has one job: let a guard, teacher or receptionist match the face to the person in about a second, often from arm's length and under poor light. That is why the rules look strict. Most organisations borrow them, loosely, from passport-photo standards such as the [ICAO guidance used for travel documents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passport_photo), because those rules exist for exactly the same reason.

Your own staff or student cards do not need to meet passport rules, but keep the core points:

- **Face straight to the camera**, head not tilted or turned, both eyes open and visible.
- **Neutral or slight closed-mouth expression.** A gentle smile is usually fine on organisation cards; a wide laugh changes the face too much.
- **Even light** with no shadow across the face and no glare on glasses.
- **Plain, light, single-colour background** with no shadow of the person on it.
- **Recent photo.** Official guidelines often ask for one under six months old; for schools, retake every year.
- **Nothing covering the face.** No sunglasses, caps or headphones. Religious head coverings are normally fine if the face from chin to forehead is fully visible, as in most government photo rules.

## The setup: background, light, distance and height

Set up one spot and keep it fixed. Every photo then matches, and the cards look like a set rather than a collection.

| Setting | What to do | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Background | Plain white, off-white or light grey wall, or white chart paper or a bedsheet taped flat | Faces separate cleanly; a plain background also looks the same in every photo |
| Person to background | About 0.5–1 m in front of the wall | Removes the dark shadow behind the head |
| Main light | Face a window or a bright, soft lamp; daylight from the side is fine if a white board reflects light onto the other cheek | Even light on the face is the single biggest quality factor |
| Light to avoid | Window or bright door behind the person; direct midday sun; one bare ceiling tube right above the head | Backlight darkens the face; overhead light puts shadows under the eyes and nose |
| Camera | Rear (main) camera, normal photo mode, flash off | The rear camera is sharper than the selfie camera; portrait or beauty modes blur hair edges and smooth skin unrealistically |
| Distance | About 1.5 m (5 ft), then zoom to 2× if the phone has it | A phone held close with a wide lens makes noses and foreheads look larger |
| Height | Lens level with the person's eyes | A low camera looks up the nose; a high one shrinks the chin |
| Stability | Lean on a table, use a cheap tripod, or rest your elbows | Blur from shake ruins more photos than low resolution |

> **Tip:** Turn on the camera's grid lines. Put the eyes on the upper horizontal line and the nose on the centre vertical line, and every photo will be framed the same way.

## How to frame the photo

Frame wider than the final crop and crop afterwards; a phone photo has pixels to spare. In the final crop, aim for:

- **Head (chin to top of hair) about 55–65% of the photo height.** Passport rules use a bigger head because their photo is larger; on a small card, a little shoulder makes the photo easier to read.
- **A small gap above the hair**, about 5–10% of the height. Do not cut off the top of the head.
- **Eyes roughly 40–45% down from the top** of the photo.
- **Face centred left to right**, shoulders level and visible.

## What size should an ID card photo be?

What matters is the shape of the crop and enough pixels for 300 DPI printing. These are typical photo boxes in common layouts; your template may differ by a millimetre or two.

| Card and layout | Photo box (mm) | Pixels at 300 DPI | Pixels at 600 DPI | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| CR80 landscape, photo on the left | 22 × 28 | 260 × 331 | 520 × 661 | about 4:5 |
| CR80 portrait, photo at the top | 26 × 32 | 307 × 378 | 614 × 756 | about 4:5 |
| CR80 portrait, large photo | 30 × 40 | 354 × 472 | 709 × 945 | 3:4 |
| 4 × 3 in event badge | 30 × 38 | 354 × 449 | 709 × 898 | about 4:5 |
| Passport-style print (for reference) | 35 × 45 | 413 × 531 | 827 × 1,063 | 7:9 |

The pixel figures come from a simple formula: **millimetres ÷ 25.4 × DPI**. For example, 26 mm ÷ 25.4 × 300 = 307 pixels. A 12-megapixel phone photo is about 4,000 × 3,000 pixels, so even after you crop down to the head and shoulders you usually keep well over 1,000 pixels of height, three times what the card needs. For every card size in millimetres, inches and pixels, see our [ID card size chart](/blog/id-card-size-cr80-mm-inches-pixels/).

### Can I use a passport-size photo for an ID card?

Yes, if you have a clean digital copy or a sharp print. The Bangladesh passport photo is usually listed as 40 × 50 mm, a 4:5 shape that crops well to a card photo box. But re-photographing a small print loses detail and adds glare, so take a fresh photo when you can.

## Step by step: taking one ID card photo

1. **Clear the background.** Move posters, plants and chairs. Tape white chart paper or a plain sheet flat on the wall if the wall is coloured or dirty.
2. **Place the chair 0.5–1 m from the wall**, facing the light. A stool is better than a swivel chair, which lets people turn.
3. **Check the person.** Hair off the eyes, collar straight, glasses clean, cap and headphones off.
4. **Hold the phone at eye level, about 1.5 m away**, rear camera, flash off, grid on. Zoom to 2× if available.
5. **Ask them to sit up straight, chin slightly down, look into the lens.** Most people tilt their chin up for photos; a tiny nod fixes it.
6. **Tap the face to focus and set exposure.** If the face looks dark, slide the exposure up a little rather than turning on the flash.
7. **Take three shots.** Blinks and half-smiles are common; picking the best of three takes seconds.
8. **Zoom in on the best shot and check it** against the checklist below before the person leaves.
9. **Label it immediately** with the person's ID number, either in the app or by renaming the file (for example `2027-0415.jpg`).
10. **Crop to the photo box ratio** when you place it on the card, keeping the head in the same position as on the other cards.

> **Tip:** For glasses, raise the phone slightly or ask the person to lower their chin a little. That moves the reflection of the window off the lenses without asking them to take their glasses off.

## Checklist: approve or retake?

Check each photo at full zoom on the phone. Retake if any of these fail:

- Face sharp, especially the eyes (no shake blur)
- Face evenly lit; no dark half, no bright shine on forehead or nose
- No shadow of the person on the background
- Background plain and light, with no objects, edges or people behind
- Head straight, not tilted or turned; shoulders level
- Both eyes open and visible; no glare on glasses
- Top of the hair not cut off, with a small gap above
- Natural colour (not yellow from a tube light or blue from shade)
- No beauty filter, portrait blur or stickers
- Correct person, labelled with the correct ID number

The last point causes more reprints than any other. Labelling at the moment of capture prevents it.

## Common mistakes and how to fix them

| Problem | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Dark face, bright background | Window or open door behind the person | Turn the setup around so the light is in front |
| Dark shadow on the wall behind the head | Person too close to the wall, or a flash | Move the chair 0.5–1 m forward; switch off the flash |
| Big nose, small ears | Phone too close with a wide lens (typical selfie) | Step back to about 1.5 m and zoom 2× |
| Shadows under the eyes | Single light straight above | Use window light from the front, or a lamp at face height |
| Yellow or green skin | Mixed light (tube light plus daylight) | Use one kind of light; switch off overhead tubes near a window |
| Grainy, soft photo | Dim room, phone raises sensitivity | Add light; do not rely on night mode |
| Every photo framed differently | Photographer moves between shots | Mark the chair and camera positions with tape |
| Cut-off hair or chin after cropping | Framed too tightly in camera | Frame wider and crop later |

Editing can fix brightness, a slight tilt or a crop, but not a shadow across the face, blur or a wrong angle. Keep edits honest: never smooth faces or change features. The card has to look like the person.

## Running a photo day for a school, factory or office

For 50 or 500 people, the setup above becomes a production line run by two people.

- **Before the day:** prepare the list as a spreadsheet with ID numbers, sorted in the order people will come (by class and section, or by department and line). Tell people the day in advance so they come in uniform or office dress with hair tidy.
- **The station:** one fixed chair, tape marks for the chair and the camera, the background taped flat, and one light setup that does not change through the day. If daylight changes a lot, choose a room with steady light or add a lamp.
- **Roles:** one person calls the next name and confirms the ID number; one person takes the photo, checks it and labels it.
- **Pace:** with a fixed setup, each photo takes well under a minute including the check. Mornings usually give softer light.
- **Absentees:** keep a list of who was missed. A dashboard that shows who still has no photo saves a scroll through hundreds of cards.
- **Privacy:** photos are personal data. Keep them on the organisation's phone or account, delete copies from personal phones afterwards and never post the set in group chats. For children, use photos only for the purpose guardians were told about.

For schools and madrasas, the photo day fits naturally into the yearly renewal; our [school ID card design guide](/blog/school-id-card-design-guide/) explains how to plan that renewal around one reusable student list. For the full workflow from list to printed card, see [how to make ID cards on your phone](/blog/how-to-make-id-cards-on-your-phone/).

### When people send their own photos

Send short instructions first: "Face a window, plain light wall behind you, someone else holds the phone at your eye level from about 1.5 m, no filters." Ask for the file as a document or at original quality, because chat apps compress photos, and check each one against the same checklist.

## Frequently asked questions

### What background is best for an ID card photo?

A plain white, off-white or light grey background works best for most organisation ID cards, because it looks clean on any template and separates the face clearly. Light blue is also common. Whatever you choose, use the same background for everyone and keep the person 0.5–1 m away from it so no shadow falls on it.

### How do I take an ID card photo with my phone at home?

Stand facing a window with a plain light wall behind you, about half a metre to a metre away from it. Ask someone to hold your phone at your eye level, about 1.5 m away, using the rear camera with the flash off and a little zoom. Take several shots, pick the sharpest one and crop it to head and shoulders.

### What size should an ID card photo be in pixels?

At 300 DPI, a typical CR80 card photo box of 22 × 28 mm to 26 × 32 mm needs about 260 × 331 to 307 × 378 pixels. Use roughly double those numbers if you print at 600 DPI. A normal phone photo cropped to head and shoulders is far larger, so you will not run short of pixels.

### Can I smile in an ID card photo?

For your own school, office or event card, a gentle closed-mouth smile is usually fine and often looks friendlier. Passport and government photos normally require a neutral expression, so follow those rules only when an official document asks for them. Keep the mouth closed and the face relaxed either way.

### Should I wear glasses in my ID card photo?

Wear them if you wear them every day, because the card should look like you on a normal day. Make sure your eyes are fully visible through clear lenses and there is no glare; lowering your chin slightly or raising the camera a little usually removes reflections. Tinted or sunglasses should be taken off.
