<b>Key takeaways</b>

- Most good ID cards use one of a few proven layouts: **header band, side stripe, corner shape, art background with a white panel, photo-left landscape, split card, minimal white and bold colour-block**. Pick the layout first, then the colours.
- Keep the **photo, name and ID number on a white or very light panel**. Put colour and pattern in the band, stripe or edges, never directly behind small text.
- Use colour pairs with a contrast ratio of **at least 4.5:1** for small text. White on navy (about 10:1) or black on amber (about 12:1) work; white on sky blue (about 3:1) or white on orange (under 3:1) do not.
- Use **one brand colour plus one neutral**, and at most one accent. Add a printed word to any colour code, because about 1 in 12 men has some colour vision deficiency.
- Extend every background **3 mm past the trim line** and keep text **3 mm inside it**, or a slightly off cut on an A4 sheet will show white slivers or clip names.
</div>

Search for ID card design ideas and you get endless template galleries, but very little on *why* some cards look sharp and readable while others look busy or cheap once printed.

This guide gives you eight layouts that work on a standard CR80 card (85.6 × 54 mm), with real measurements, colour combinations with contrast ratios we calculated, rules for backgrounds, and a checklist to run before you print a whole office or school.

## Start with the job of the card, not the decoration

An ID card is read in about two seconds, usually by a guard, a receptionist or a teacher at a gate. In that time they need to match the **photo** to the face, read the **name**, and see the **organisation** and **role or category**. Everything else is secondary.

So every design idea below follows the same order of importance:

1. Photo (largest element after the background)
2. Name (largest text, bold)
3. Role, class or category (often shown by colour *and* a word)
4. Organisation name and logo
5. ID number, validity and smaller details

If a design idea makes any of these harder to see, drop it, however good it looks on screen. Our guide to [employee ID card design and what to include](/blog/employee-id-card-design-what-to-include/) covers the fields and font sizes in detail; this article is about layout, colour and style.

### 1. Header band (the safe classic)

A solid colour band across the top, about **18–22 mm tall** on a portrait card, holds the logo and organisation name in white. The photo (about 26 × 32 mm) sits centred below it, then name, role and ID number. A thin 4–6 mm band at the bottom can carry the category word or validity.

It suits almost everyone: offices, schools, madrasas, hospitals. It also prints well on home inkjets because most of the card is white.

### 2. Side stripe

A vertical stripe **6–10 mm wide** down the left edge carries the colour code, with the category printed sideways inside it (STUDENT, STAFF, CONTRACTOR). Because the stripe is the only coloured area, you can switch colours per category without changing anything else.

This is a good idea for schools with one colour per class group, or factories with one colour per staff type.

### 3. Corner shape or curve

A curved or diagonal shape fills one or two corners instead of a straight band. It looks more modern than a header band with the same amount of ink. Keep the curve away from the photo and name; a shape that cuts under the text makes it harder to read. A round or rounded photo frame pairs well with this style.

### 4. Art background with a white panel

A pattern, gradient or illustration fills the whole card, and a white panel inset by **4–5 mm** on each side holds the photo and text. You get the "colourful" look people search for, and the text stays readable because it never sits on the artwork.

This works well for events, clubs, coaching centres and creative businesses. On inkjet printers, check one test sheet first: a full-bleed background uses a lot of ink and can show banding.

### 5. Photo-left landscape

The classic landscape card: photo about **22 × 28 mm** on the left, name and details stacked on the right, a header band with the logo across the top. It fits wallets and horizontal holders, and gives long names more room on a single line.

### 6. Split card

The card is divided into two zones: a coloured left third (landscape) or top third (portrait) with the photo set into it, and a white two-thirds with the text. Put a thin white border around the photo so it does not merge with the colour.

### 7. Minimal white

Almost entirely white, with the logo, a small accent line (1–2 mm) in the brand colour and generous spacing. It looks premium and is the cheapest to print. It needs good typography to work: one clean font, a clear size difference between name and details, and nothing crowded into the corners.

### 8. Bold colour block (visitor and event passes)

The whole front is one strong colour with a huge word, such as VISITOR, CREW or GUEST, readable from several metres. This is not for staff cards with photos, but it is the right idea for passes where the category matters more than the person.

## Which layout fits which organisation?

| Layout | Best for | Ink use on A4 inkjet | Readability risk | Works in portrait and landscape |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Header band | Offices, schools, hospitals | Low | Very low | Yes |
| Side stripe | Schools, factories with colour codes | Very low | Very low | Yes (stripe on left or top) |
| Corner shape | Modern offices, startups, NGOs | Low | Low if curve avoids text | Yes |
| Art background + white panel | Events, clubs, coaching centres | High | Low (text on panel) | Yes |
| Photo-left landscape | Corporate staff, wallets | Low | Very low | Landscape only |
| Split card | Universities, agencies | Medium | Low | Yes |

| Bold colour block | Visitor, event, crew passes | High | Very low for one word | Yes |

## ID card colour combinations that stay readable

Colour combination is where most homemade cards go wrong: light text on a mid-tone colour that looked fine on a bright phone screen turns muddy on paper.

We checked common pairs with the WCAG contrast formula, which compares the brightness of two colours on a scale from 1:1 (no difference) to 21:1 (black on white). The [W3C guidance](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html) asks for at least **4.5:1 for normal text** and **3:1 for large text**. It was written for screens, but it is a useful floor for small printed text too, and printed colours usually lose a little contrast compared with the screen.

| Text on background | Example hex | Contrast (our calculation) | Use for small text? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Black on white | #111111 on #FFFFFF | 18.9:1 | Yes, the best |
| Black on pale blue | #111111 on #E8F1FF | 16.6:1 | Yes |
| Black on amber/yellow | #111111 on #FFC107 | 11.6:1 | Yes |
| White on maroon | #FFFFFF on #800000 | 10.9:1 | Yes |
| White on navy | #FFFFFF on #1F3A93 | 10.1:1 | Yes |
| White on purple | #FFFFFF on #7B1FA2 | 8.2:1 | Yes |
| Black on orange | #111111 on #F57C00 | 7.0:1 | Yes |
| Black on sky blue | #111111 on #00A3E0 | 6.6:1 | Yes |
| White on bottle green | #FFFFFF on #0B6E4F | 6.3:1 | Yes |
| White on red | #FFFFFF on #C62828 | 5.6:1 | Yes, in bold |
| Mid grey on white | #6B6B6B on #FFFFFF | 5.3:1 | OK for labels only |
| White on sky blue | #FFFFFF on #00A3E0 | 2.9:1 | No, large bold words only |
| Light grey on white | #999999 on #FFFFFF | 2.8:1 | No |
| White on orange | #FFFFFF on #F57C00 | 2.7:1 | No |
| White on amber/yellow | #FFFFFF on #FFC107 | 1.6:1 | Never |

Two patterns stand out. **Dark colours (navy, maroon, purple, bottle green) take white text; bright colours (yellow, amber, orange, sky blue) need black text.** Many school cards print white names on a bright orange or light blue band and become hard to read; switching the text to black fixes it without changing the brand colour.

### A simple colour scheme formula

- **One brand colour** for the band, stripe or shapes (taken from the logo).
- **One neutral** for text: near-black (#111111 to #222222) on white.
- **One accent at most**, used small: a line, the category word, an icon.

Popular, safe schemes we see in offices and schools in Bangladesh: navy + white + gold accent, bottle green + white (common for schools and madrasas), maroon + cream, and teal + white + dark grey. For colour-coded categories, keep the layout identical and change only the band colour, always with the category printed as a word.

> **Tip:** About 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women have some form of colour vision deficiency, according to [Colour Blind Awareness](https://www.colourblindawareness.org/colour-blindness/). Never use red vs green as the only difference between two categories.

## ID card background ideas: what works and what does not

Backgrounds are the most requested design idea and the most common cause of unreadable cards.

**Backgrounds that work**

- **Soft gradients** between two shades of the brand colour, behind a white text panel.
- **Geometric patterns** (lines, dots, hexagons) at low contrast, in the band or frame only.
- **Light tint** of the brand colour (5–10% strength) across the whole card, with dark text. This gives colour without hurting contrast.
- **Faint watermark logo** behind the text, at around 5–8% opacity, which also makes casual copying a little harder to hide.

**Backgrounds to avoid**

- Photos of buildings, landscapes or the campus behind text. They look good in a mock-up and turn into noise at 6–8 pt.
- Strong patterns or textures directly behind the name or ID number.
- Very dark full backgrounds on home inkjets: heavy ink, longer drying, and more risk of smudging when cards are laminated.
- Low-resolution images pulled from the web. At 300 DPI, a full portrait CR80 background needs about 638 × 1011 pixels, or about 708 × 1082 with 3 mm bleed.

Whatever background you choose, extend it **3 mm past the trim line** (bleed) and keep all text and the photo **3 mm inside** it. When you cut ten cards from an A4 sheet by hand, cuts drift by a millimetre or two, and bleed hides that.

## Modern ID card design: what makes a card look current

"Modern" mostly means less, not more. Cards that look current in 2026 share a few traits:

- **More white space.** Fewer boxes and borders, bigger margins, details aligned on one left edge or centred, not both.
- **Clean sans-serif fonts** in two weights (regular and bold). Inter, Roboto, Poppins or Noto Sans for English; Hind Siliguri or Noto Sans Bengali for Bangla.
- **Rounded photo frames or soft corners** that echo the card's own rounded corners (about 3.2 mm radius).
- **Flat colour** instead of glossy effects, drop shadows and bevels.
- **A QR code on the back** rather than the front, keeping the front uncluttered.
- **Consistent front and back**: the same band colour and logo position on both sides.

What dates a card quickly: clip-art borders, Comic Sans or script fonts for names, rainbow gradients behind text, and the organisation name in outlined or 3D letters.

## How to turn a design idea into a finished card

1. **Pick one layout** from the eight above based on who checks the card and how you print.
2. **Take the brand colour from your logo** and test it against the contrast table. Decide whether text on that colour is white or black.
3. **Choose portrait or landscape**: portrait for lanyards, landscape for wallets and clips.
4. **Place the fixed elements**: logo, band or stripe, photo frame, field positions.
5. **Test with your longest name** and a real photo, not a placeholder.
6. **Design the back** in the same style: return text, validity, signature, QR code.
7. **Print one test sheet** at 100% scale on the paper or PVC you will actually use, cut it and check it at arm's length and in the holder.
8. **Only then print the whole batch.**

## Checklist before you approve an ID card design

1. Photo, name and ID number sit on a white or very light area.
2. Small text has a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background.
3. No more than one brand colour, one neutral and one accent.
4. Any colour code also has a printed word (STAFF, VISITOR, CLASS 6).
5. Red and green are not the only difference between two categories.
6. One font family, at most three sizes; name is the largest text.
7. Background extends 3 mm past the trim; text stays 3 mm inside it.
8. Background images are at least 300 DPI at print size.
9. The longest name fits without touching the photo or edge.
10. Front and back use the same colours and logo position.
11. One test sheet printed, cut and checked in a real holder.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best colour for an ID card?

There is no single best colour; use your organisation's brand colour for the band or stripe and keep the text area white. Dark brand colours such as navy, maroon, purple or bottle green work with white text, while bright colours such as yellow, amber or orange need black text to stay readable.

### What are good colour combinations for an ID card?

Navy with white and a gold accent, bottle green with white, maroon with cream, and teal with white and dark grey are reliable choices. Check that any text on a coloured area has a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1; white on yellow, orange or light blue usually fails.

### How do I make an ID card look modern?

Use more white space, one clean sans-serif font in two weights, flat colours instead of shadows or bevels, and a simple curve or corner shape instead of heavy borders. Move the QR code and small print to the back so the front only shows the photo, name, role and logo.

### Can I use a photo or picture as an ID card background?

You can, but keep it out from behind the text. Put the picture in the header band or around the edges, or place a white panel over it for the photo and details. Make sure the image is sharp at 300 DPI at the card's print size.

### Should an ID card be portrait or landscape?

Portrait cards hang straight on lanyards and are common in schools, hospitals and events; landscape cards suit wallets, clips and horizontal holders. Every layout in this guide except photo-left works in both orientations; our [portrait vs landscape ID card comparison](/blog/portrait-vs-landscape-id-card/) has the measurements, slot positions and a decision table.

### How many colours should an ID card have?

Two or three: one brand colour, one neutral for text, and at most one small accent. If you colour-code categories, change only the band or stripe colour and keep the rest of the card identical.
