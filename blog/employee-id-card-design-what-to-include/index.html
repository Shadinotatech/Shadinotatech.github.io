Employee ID Card Design: What to Include, Front and Back

Employee ID card design made simple: which fields go on the front and back, what never to print, font sizes in pt and a layout for every company size.

By Shadinota Tech Team18 November 2026 · Updated 19 November 202613 min read

Key takeaways

  • The front of an employee ID card needs only six things: logo and company name, photo, full name, designation, department and employee ID number. Everything else goes on the back.
  • The back carries validity date, blood group, an emergency or office phone, "if found, please return" text and the authorised signature.
  • Never print home address, national ID or passport number, date of birth, salary grade or personal mobile numbers on a staff card. Whoever finds a lost card should be able to return it, not misuse it.
  • Make the hierarchy obvious: photo and name first (name 10–12 pt), designation second (8–9 pt), the rest 6.5–8 pt, never below 6 pt on a CR80 card.
  • Use one layout for everyone and change only a colour band or label for departments, contractors or access levels, so a guard can read the card in two seconds.

A good employee ID card design answers one question fast: is this person who they say they are, and do they belong here? Most office ID cards fail because they try to answer ten questions at once, with crowded text, tiny fonts and personal details that should never be printed.

This guide gives you a field-by-field plan for the front and back of a staff ID card, a list of what to leave off, exact font and element sizes for a standard CR80 card, and layouts for a ten-person office, a mid-size company and a large factory. Everything below assumes the standard 85.6 × 54 mm card; if you need other sizes, see our ID card size chart in mm, inches and pixels.

What should be on an employee ID card?

The card is checked by a guard, a receptionist or a colleague who has a few seconds at most. So the front holds only what identifies the person and the organisation.

Field Side Required? Why it is there Practical note
Company logo and name Front Yes Shows which organisation issued the card Logo 8–12 mm tall in a header band
Photo Front Yes The real identity check About 22 × 28 mm (landscape) or 26 × 32 mm (portrait), plain background
Full name Front Yes Who the person is Largest text on the card, 10–12 pt
Designation (job title) Front Yes Tells others what the person does Use the official title from the appointment letter
Department or section Front Usually Helps reception and guards route people Short form is fine: "Accounts", "QC", "Line 4"
Employee ID number Front Yes Links the card to HR records Use the same number as payroll; 6.5–8 pt, bold
Validity / expiry date Back (or front) Recommended Stops old cards being used after someone leaves Put it on the front if guards must check it daily
Blood group Back Common in South Asia Useful in an emergency Only print what the employee has confirmed
Emergency or office phone Back Recommended Who to call if the holder is hurt or the card is found Use the company number, not a personal one
"If found, please return" text Back Recommended Gets lost cards back to you One or two lines with the office address
Authorised signature Back (or front) Recommended Shows the card was issued by someone with authority Transparent signature over a printed name and title
QR code or barcode Back Optional Attendance, access or quick lookup At least 15 × 15 mm for a QR code

Front vs back: where each field goes

A simple rule keeps the front clean: if a guard does not need it to let the person in, it goes on the back. The front is for recognition; the back is for reference and recovery.

Portrait or landscape?

Both work. Portrait is better on a lanyard because the card hangs straight and the photo sits at eye level. Landscape is better in a wallet, a clip holder or an access-card sleeve, and it gives long names more room. Pick one orientation for the whole organisation so cards look consistent. If your people mostly wear lanyards, choose portrait and put the photo above the name; the field order stays the same.

Single-sided or double-sided?

A single-sided card is acceptable for a small team, but you then have to squeeze validity and a return address onto the front. Once you print the back anyway (it costs one more pass through the printer, or one more A4 sheet), use it. Our guide to printing ID cards on A4 paper shows how to line up the front and back sheets so they match after cutting.

What not to print on a staff ID card

Every ID card gets lost eventually. Design as if the next person to hold it is a stranger. The test for each field is simple: would it harm the employee or the company if a stranger read it?

Do not print Why What to do instead
Home address Tells a stranger where the holder lives Print the office address for returns
National ID, passport or tax number Can be used for fraud and identity theft Keep it in HR records, linked by employee ID
Date of birth Combined with name and photo, it helps impersonation Not needed for identification at the gate
Personal mobile number Invites unwanted calls if the card is lost Print the office or HR phone
Salary grade, pay band or bank details Private, and irrelevant to identification Leave it in payroll
Door codes, passwords or Wi-Fi keys Anyone who finds the card gets in Share them separately and change them regularly
Religion, caste or marital status Sensitive and not needed to identify someone Leave it off entirely
Medical conditions beyond blood group Health data needs consent and care Keep detailed emergency information with HR

Some roles need extra care with names. Staff who deal with the public, such as nurses, counter staff or security guards, can be targeted after an argument. Several hospital systems let staff choose to leave their last name off the badge; Penn State Health is one published example. If you allow this, the full name stays in your records and the employee ID number still links the card to the person.

Tip: If you add a QR code, encode the employee ID number or a link to a verification page that you control, not the person's full personal record. A QR code is just text that anyone with a phone can read.

Visual hierarchy: what people should read first

People read an ID card in a fixed order: photo, name, role, organisation. Your design should follow that order rather than fight it. On a CR80 card, these sizes have worked well in our templates:

Element Size on a CR80 card Weight
Full name 10–12 pt Bold
Designation 8–9 pt Regular or medium
Department, ID number, site 6.5–8 pt ID number bold
Back-side text 6.5–7.5 pt Regular
Minimum for anything 6 pt Avoid thin weights at this size

A few rules make the difference between a card that reads at arm's length and one that does not:

  • One font family, two or three sizes. A clean sans-serif (Inter, Roboto, Noto Sans, or Hind Siliguri for Bangla) in regular and bold is enough. Decorative fonts make names hard to check.
  • Dark text on a light area. Keep the photo and text on a white or very light panel. Put colour in the header band and edges, not behind the name.
  • Plan for long names. Test your layout with the longest name in your staff list. For names like "Mohammad Abdur Rahman Chowdhury", allow two lines or a slightly smaller size instead of letting the text run into the photo.
  • Leave the safe zone empty. Keep text and the photo at least 3 mm from every edge, so small cutting errors do not trim them.

Staff ID card format by company size

The right format depends less on your industry than on how many people you card, how often people join and leave, and who checks the cards.

Small office (up to 25) Mid-size company (25–300) Factory or multi-site (300+)
Front Logo, photo, name, designation, ID Add department Add department, section or line, and site
Back Return text, office phone, signature Add validity and blood group Add validity, blood group, emergency phone, QR or barcode
Colour coding Not needed By department or staff type By staff type or access zone, with a text label
Validity Optional 1–2 years 1 year, or contract end date for temporary staff
ID number Simple serial (001, 002) Year joined + serial Site or department code + year + serial
Who checks Everyone knows everyone Reception Guards at gates, often at shift change

For factories, the employee ID number and the name should be readable from a metre away, because guards check hundreds of cards in a few minutes. A bold 8 pt ID number and a coloured band for each staff category (worker, staff, management, contractor) speed this up a lot.

Colour-coding departments and access levels

Colour is the fastest signal on a card, so use it for one thing only: staff type (permanent, contract, intern, contractor), access zone (office, production floor, store) or department. Pick one and stick to it. Use five or six colours at most, and always add a text label inside the colour band ("CONTRACTOR", "PRODUCTION"), because about one in twelve men has some form of colour vision deficiency and colours also fade or print differently on cheaper printers. Avoid red and green as the only difference between two categories.

Ready-to-copy text for the back of the card

You can copy and adapt these lines. Replace the parts in square brackets.

This card is the property of [Company Name] and must be shown on request. It is not transferable. If found, please return it to [office address] or call [office phone].

Valid until: [31 December 2027] · Blood group: [B+] · Emergency: [office or HR phone]

Keep the back to four or five lines plus the signature; longer rules belong in the staff handbook.

Employee ID cards in Bangladesh: law and practice

In Bangladesh, an ID card is not just good practice for most employers. Section 5 of the Bangladesh Labour Act 2006 says no employer may employ a worker without a letter of appointment, and that every worker must be given an identity card with a photograph. The Act sets the duty, not a design, so the layout is up to you.

Practice in Bangladesh differs from many Western guides in a few ways:

  • Blood group is expected. Most offices, factories and schools print it, usually on the back. Print it only after the employee confirms it, ideally from a test report, because a wrong blood group is worse than none.
  • Bangla and English together. Many factories print the name in both scripts, especially for workers who read Bangla more easily. Keep the English spelling identical to payroll and bank records, and use a proper Bangla font so conjuncts render correctly.
  • Section and line matter. In RMG factories, "Sewing, Line 7" or "Finishing" on the front tells a supervisor at once whether someone is in the right place.
  • Printing is usually on A4. Most small and mid-size organisations print cards on A4 sheets at a local print shop, then laminate and cut them, rather than buying a card printer.

Checklist before you approve the design

  1. The front shows only logo and company name, photo, full name, designation, department and employee ID.
  2. The name is the largest text and is readable at arm's length (10–12 pt).
  3. Nothing on the card is smaller than 6 pt, and nothing sits within 3 mm of the edge.
  4. The photo is sharp, on a plain background, and about 22 × 28 mm (landscape) or 26 × 32 mm (portrait).
  5. No home address, national ID number, date of birth, salary information or personal phone number appears anywhere.
  6. The back has validity, emergency or office phone, return text and an authorised signature.
  7. Colour bands, if used, mean one thing only and carry a text label.
  8. The longest name in the list fits without overlapping the photo or the edge.
  9. Employee ID numbers match payroll exactly, with no duplicates.
  10. A printed proof at 100% scale measures 85.6 × 54 mm and the back lines up with the front.

Frequently asked questions

What information should be on an employee ID card?

The front should show the company logo and name, the employee's photo, full name, designation, department and employee ID number. The back should carry the validity date, an emergency or office phone number, "if found, please return" text and an authorised signature. Blood group is common in Bangladesh and South Asia and usually goes on the back.

What should you not put on an employee ID card?

Leave off anything that would help a stranger harm the employee or the company if the card is lost: home address, national ID or passport number, date of birth, personal mobile number, salary details, door codes and sensitive data such as religion or medical conditions. Keep those in HR records and link them to the card through the employee ID number.

What is the standard size of an office ID card?

Most office ID cards use the CR80 size, 85.6 × 54 mm (3.370 × 2.125 in), the same as a bank card. It fits standard holders, lanyards and wallet slots. At 300 DPI the design file is 1011 × 638 pixels, or a little larger if you add bleed for cutting.

Should an employee ID card show the full name?

For most offices and factories, yes: the full name matches payroll and makes identification clear. For staff who deal with the public, such as nurses or counter staff, some employers allow first name and last initial only. The employee ID number still links the card to the full record.

Should the employee ID card be portrait or landscape?

Choose portrait if staff wear cards on lanyards, because the card hangs straight with the photo at the top. Choose landscape for wallets, clip holders and access-card sleeves, or when many names are long. Use the same orientation for everyone so cards are easy to check.

Is an ID card mandatory for employees in Bangladesh?

Yes, for workers covered by the Bangladesh Labour Act 2006. Section 5 requires the employer to give every worker a letter of appointment and an identity card with a photograph. The Act does not prescribe a design, so the fields and layout in this guide are a practical standard, not a legal format.
