Event Badge Design: Sizes, Font Sizes and a Layout That Works

Event badge design that people can read across a room: badge sizes in mm and inches, name font sizes, colour-coded roles, QR check-in and A4 print layouts.

By Md. Ruhul Amin24 November 202613 min read

Key takeaways

  • Good event badge design puts the first name first and biggest, readable at 2–3 m. On a 4 × 3 in (101.6 × 76.2 mm) badge that means a name of about 40–60 pt; everything else is smaller.
  • The 4 × 3 inch landscape badge is the everyday conference size and prints 6 per A4 sheet. Use 4 × 6 in or A6 when the badge also carries a schedule or sponsors.
  • Show the attendee type (Speaker, Volunteer, Media) as a coloured band with the word printed on it, never colour alone.
  • A check-in QR code needs about 2–2.5 cm to scan reliably at desk distance. Encode only a registration number, not a phone number or email.
  • Badges flip on lanyards. Print the same front on both sides, or use two holes at the top corners.

Most event badges fail at the one job they have: telling the person in front of you who you are. The event logo is huge, the name is small, and half the badges hang backwards.

This guide gives you the numbers to get event badge design right the first time: which size to use, how big the name must be, how to colour-code roles, where the QR code goes, and how many badges fit on an A4 sheet. It works for conferences, seminars, job fairs, university fests and corporate events alike.

What should an event badge show (and in what order)?

A badge is read in about two seconds, often from an angle, so the order of information matters more than the decoration. Design from the top down in this priority:

  1. First name, large. This is the word people say out loud.
  2. Last name, smaller (about 50–60% of the first name), or on the same line if names are short.
  3. Organisation or designation, one line.
  4. Attendee type, as a coloured band with a word: Delegate, Speaker, Volunteer, Media, Organiser, Exhibitor.
  5. Event name and logo, small, at the top or bottom edge. Attendees already know which event they are at.
  6. QR code or registration number, bottom corner, for check-in and session scanning.

Leave off anything that does not help at the venue: home address, full phone number, date of birth. If the organisers need to reach someone, that belongs in the registration list, not on a badge hanging round their neck.

Tip: Keep the name in the top half of the badge. Lanyards pull badges down and tables hide the bottom third when people sit.

Which event badge size should you use?

The four sizes below cover almost every event. All dimensions are the trimmed size; the A4 counts assume a 5 mm printer margin, which is typical for home and office printers.

Size mm Inches Per A4 sheet Best for
CR80 card 85.6 × 54 3.37 × 2.13 10 Multi-day passes, staff at events, reusable badges in card holders
4 × 3 badge 101.6 × 76.2 4 × 3 6 (2 × 3) Standard conferences, seminars, trade fairs
3R 88.9 × 127 3.5 × 5 4 (2 × 2) Portrait passes on lanyards, photo-paper printing
4R / 4 × 6 101.6 × 152.4 4 × 6 2 Badges that carry a schedule, map or sponsor logos
A6 105 × 148 4.13 × 5.83 4 (A4 cut in quarters) Large portrait passes, festival and concert passes

How we got those A4 numbers: an A4 sheet is 210 × 297 mm. Two 4 × 3 badges side by side take 203.2 mm of width, and three rows take 228.6 mm of height, so 6 fit with room for cut marks. A 4R badge is 152.4 mm tall, so only one row fits (two rows would need 304.8 mm). A6 is exactly a quarter of A4, so four fit with no gap; keep all text at least 5 mm inside every edge, because the outer border falls in the printer's unprintable margin.

For exact pixel sizes at 300 and 600 DPI, see our ID card and badge size chart. If you are unsure, choose 4 × 3 landscape: pouches and holders for it are easy to buy, and it gives the name enough width.

How big should the name be on an event badge?

Font size is where most badges go wrong, so here is the reasoning in numbers.

A point is 1/72 inch, or 0.353 mm. Capital letters are roughly 70% of the point size, so a 36 pt name has capitals about 8.9 mm tall. Badge suppliers such as NameBadge recommend 24–36 pt for names read at 1–2 m (3–6 ft), and the conference platform Exordo argues for first names of 72 pt or more so they can be read from about 4.5 m.

Put together, a practical working rule (our own estimate from those figures) is about 1 mm of capital height for every 20–25 cm of reading distance. Conversations at events happen at 1–3 m, so aim for capitals of 8–14 mm.

Text 4 × 3 badge CR80 card 4R / A6 portrait
First name 40–60 pt 20–28 pt 54–72 pt
Last name 22–30 pt 12–16 pt 28–36 pt
Organisation / designation 14–18 pt 8–10 pt 16–20 pt
Role band text 14–18 pt, bold caps 8–9 pt, bold caps 18–24 pt, bold caps
Event name 10–14 pt 7–8 pt 12–16 pt

Worked example: long names

On a 4 × 3 badge with 6 mm side margins, the name line has 89.6 mm of width. In a regular sans-serif font, an average letter is a little over half the point size wide, so at 54 pt (19 mm em) each letter takes about 10 mm. That fits a first name of 8–9 letters, such as "Nusrat" or "Tanvir", but not "Mohammad Abdullah".

So set a rule before printing:

  • Print the first name alone on line one whenever possible.
  • Let long names shrink to fit, but never below about 36 pt on a 4 × 3 badge.
  • If a name is still too long, move the family name to line two rather than squeezing letters.

Bangla script needs a little extra size: at the same point size, Bangla letters look smaller and the vowel signs above and below the line are easy to lose. As a rule of thumb, set Bangla names about 10–15% larger than the English line, and test print one badge before the run.

Fonts, colours and contrast

  • Use a plain sans-serif for names (for example Inter, Roboto, Open Sans or Noto Sans, which also has a Bangla version). Script and condensed fonts look elegant on screen and unreadable at 2 m.
  • Dark text on a light background for the name zone. Full-bleed photos or busy patterns behind the name cut readability sharply.
  • Aim for strong contrast. The web accessibility guideline WCAG asks for at least 4.5:1 for normal text; on a badge, black or very dark navy on white comfortably exceeds it.
  • Keep the event's brand colour for the top band and the role band, not for the name.
  • Matte paper or a matte pouch avoids glare under hall lighting, which makes glossy badges unreadable at certain angles.

How do you colour-code attendee types?

Colour-coding lets security, volunteers and speakers recognise each other at a glance. Keep it simple and consistent:

Role Suggested band colour Printed word
Delegate / participant Blue DELEGATE
Speaker / guest Teal or green SPEAKER
Organiser / staff Red or orange ORGANISER
Volunteer Yellow VOLUNTEER
Media / press Purple MEDIA
Exhibitor / sponsor Grey or black EXHIBITOR

Two rules matter more than the exact colours. First, always print the word as well: about 1 in 12 men has some form of colour vision deficiency, and red–green confusion is the most common. Second, limit yourself to five or six colours. Beyond that, people stop remembering what each one means. Brief the volunteers and the security desk with a one-page legend.

For access control (backstage, VIP lounge, exam hall), add a large letter or symbol to the band, such as A for all-access. A guard reads a letter faster than a colour shade under dim lighting.

Where should the QR code go, and how big?

A QR code on the badge turns check-in into a two-second scan and lets you count attendance per session. Getting it to scan reliably is mostly about size and quiet space:

  • Size: a common guideline is a scan distance of about 10 times the code's width, with 2 × 2 cm as a practical minimum. At a check-in desk the phone or scanner is 15–30 cm away, so 2–2.5 cm works well; 3 cm if volunteers will scan badges still hanging on lanyards.
  • Quiet zone: leave a plain white margin of at least four modules (the small squares) around the code. Do not let the role band colour run under it.
  • Content: encode a registration number (for example DTS26-0142), not a phone number, email or full profile. The number links to your attendee list; a lost badge then reveals nothing personal.
  • Placement: bottom right on landscape badges, bottom centre on portrait passes. Never in the top half where the name belongs.
  • Contrast: black on white only. Coloured or inverted QR codes fail more often on cheap phone cameras.

If you print a registration number under the code in small text, staff can still check people in by typing it when a scanner fails.

How do you stop badges flipping round?

A badge on a single-point lanyard spins, and half the room ends up showing a blank back. Three fixes, from cheapest to best:

  1. Print both sides the same. The name, role band and QR appear on the front and back, so it does not matter which side faces out. This is the cheapest fix for paper badges.
  2. Use two holes or slots at the top corners with a two-point lanyard or clip. The badge then hangs flat.
  3. Use a holder or pouch with two lanyard slots, which also protects paper badges in the rain or a crowded hall.

If you do use the back for something different, put information people check while wearing the badge: the Wi-Fi password, the session timetable, the venue map or an emergency number. Our guide on what to put on the back of an ID card has ready-to-copy text you can adapt.

Printing event badges on A4: step by step

Most small and medium events in Bangladesh print badges on A4 at the office or at a nearby print shop, then cut and slide them into pouches. A run of 300 badges on 4 × 3 needs 50 A4 sheets per side.

  1. Export the attendee list from your registration form (Google Forms, a ticketing site or a spreadsheet) with columns for first name, last name, organisation, role and registration number. Our guide to making ID cards from an Excel list covers clean columns and avoiding broken numbers.
  2. Sort by role, so all Speaker badges print together and you can check the band colour at a glance.
  3. Print one test sheet at 100% scale ("Actual size", never "Fit to page") and measure one badge with a ruler: it should be 101.6 × 76.2 mm.
  4. Check readability by holding the test badge at arm's length and then across a table, about 2 m away.
  5. Choose the stock: 200–250 gsm matte card for pouches; 300 gsm if badges hang without a holder. Photo paper works for 3R and 4R passes printed at a studio.
  6. Print the full run, then cut along the cut marks with a paper trimmer. For details on margins and cut marks, see how to print ID cards on A4 paper.
  7. Sort alphabetically by first name into trays at the registration desk, with a separate tray for speakers and VIPs.
  8. Print 5–10% blank or "walk-in" badges with the role band and event name, for on-the-spot registrations and misspelled names.

Event badge design checklist

Run through this before you send anything to print:

  • First name is the largest text and sits in the top half.
  • Name is at least 40 pt on a 4 × 3 badge (36 pt minimum for shrink-to-fit long names).
  • Event name and logo are smaller than the name.
  • Every role has a band colour and a printed word; a legend is ready for volunteers.
  • QR code is at least 2 cm, black on white, with a quiet zone, and encodes only a registration number.
  • No personal phone numbers, emails or home addresses on the badge.
  • Front and back are identical, or the badge uses two top holes.
  • Test sheet printed at 100% scale and measured.
  • Spelling checked against the registration list, including Bangla names.
  • 5–10% spare walk-in badges printed.

Frequently asked questions

What is the standard size for an event badge?

The most common conference badge is 4 × 3 inches (101.6 × 76.2 mm), usually landscape, and pouches for it are widely sold. Larger events use 4 × 6 inches when the badge carries a schedule or sponsor logos, while multi-day passes are often CR80 cards (85.6 × 54 mm) in a card holder.

What font size should a name be on a badge?

Aim for capitals about 8–14 mm tall so the name can be read at 1–3 m. On a 4 × 3 badge that is roughly 40–60 pt for the first name; on a CR80 card, 20–28 pt. Use a plain sans-serif font and dark text on a light background.

Should event badges have first name and last name?

Yes, but give the first name priority: print it larger, on its own line, because that is what people use in conversation. The last name can be about half the size underneath. For formal government or academic events, some organisers print the full name on one line with a title, which is fine if it still fits at 36 pt or more.

How many 4 × 3 badges fit on an A4 sheet?

Six. Two badges side by side take 203.2 mm of the sheet's 210 mm width, and three rows take 228.6 mm of its 297 mm height, leaving room for cut marks. Always print at 100% scale so each badge stays exactly 4 × 3 inches.

What should a QR code on an event badge contain?

Only a short registration or ticket number that links to your attendee list. Avoid putting phone numbers, emails or other personal data in the code, because anyone with a phone camera can read it. Print the same number in small text under the code as a backup for manual check-in.
