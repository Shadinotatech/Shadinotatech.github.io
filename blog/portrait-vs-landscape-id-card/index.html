Portrait vs Landscape ID Card: Which Orientation to Choose

Portrait vs landscape ID card compared with real CR80 measurements: lanyards, holders, slot punch position, photo size, A4 cards per sheet and a decision table.

By Md. Ruhul Amin27 November 202612 min read

Key takeaways

  • Choose by how the card is carried. Cards on a lanyard or badge reel work best in portrait (vertical), because they hang straight with the photo at chest height. Cards kept in a wallet, a sleeve or a clip holder work best in landscape (horizontal).
  • The size does not change. A CR80 card is 85.6 × 54 mm either way; portrait is simply 54 mm wide and 85.6 mm tall (638 × 1011 px at 300 DPI).
  • Portrait gives a bigger photo (about 26 × 32 mm against 22 × 28 mm on a typical landscape layout). Landscape gives a wider text line and fits 10 cards per A4 sheet instead of 9.
  • Match the holder and the slot to the orientation. The slot punch goes on the short edge for portrait cards and the long edge for landscape ones, and holders are sold as vertical or horizontal.
  • Use one orientation for each card type across the organisation. A different orientation can even work as a deliberate signal, for example portrait for staff and landscape for visitors.

Should your ID cards be vertical or horizontal? It looks like a style choice, but orientation decides how the card hangs, which holder you buy, where the lanyard slot goes, how big the photo can be and how many cards you get from each A4 sheet.

This guide compares portrait vs landscape ID cards with real CR80 measurements, shows both layouts drawn to scale, and ends with a decision table you can use for an office, school, factory or event.

Portrait vs landscape ID card: the short answer

Portrait (vertical) is the better default when people wear the card: on a lanyard, a badge reel or a neck strap. It hangs straight, the photo sits at the top where a guard looks first, and the card looks like the person's face shape rather than a business card.

Landscape (horizontal) is the better default when people carry the card: in a wallet, a phone case, a card sleeve or a clip holder on a pocket. It matches bank cards and most access cards, and it gives long names and addresses more room.

If you cannot decide, ask one question: where will the card be when someone checks it? If the answer is "hanging on the chest", go portrait. If it is "taken out of a pocket and shown", go landscape.

Does orientation change the ID card size?

No. The standard ID card, CR80 (ISO/IEC 7810 ID-1), is 85.6 × 54 mm (3.37 × 2.13 in) with rounded corners of about 3.2 mm, whichever way you turn it. A portrait card is 54 mm wide and 85.6 mm tall.

Landscape (horizontal) Portrait (vertical)
Size in mm 85.6 × 54 54 × 85.6
Pixels at 300 DPI 1011 × 638 638 × 1011
With 3 mm bleed at 300 DPI 1082 × 709 709 × 1082
Safe area (3 mm inside trim) 79.6 × 48 mm 48 × 79.6 mm

So you never need a different card stock, PVC card or printer for portrait. You only need the right design file, the right holder and the slot in the right place. Our ID card size chart lists every other format (CR79, CR100, 4 × 3 in badges, 3R, 4R, A6) in mm, inches and pixels.

How the two layouts compare, to scale

Both cards have exactly the same area, but they spend it differently. These are the numbers from the typical layouts above (our measurements, not a standard):

Measure Portrait Landscape
Photo size about 26 × 32 mm about 22 × 28 mm
Photo share of the card about 18% about 13%
Width for the name line 48 mm (full safe width) about 54 mm beside the photo, 79.6 mm if the name runs under the header
Characters per line at 10 pt bold (estimate) about 24 about 27 beside the photo
Header band height 18–22 mm 10–13 mm
Lines of detail that fit comfortably 3–4 below the name 4–5 beside the photo

The character counts are our estimate for a typical sans-serif font such as Roboto or Noto Sans, where an average bold letter is a little over half the font size wide. Your font will vary, so always test with your longest real name.

The practical takeaway: portrait wins on face recognition, landscape wins on text. A school that mostly needs to match a child to a photo benefits from portrait. An office card that carries a long designation, department and a two-line company name is easier to lay out in landscape.

Vertical ID cards: when portrait is the right choice

Choose portrait when most of these are true:

  • Cards are worn on lanyards or badge reels all day: schools, madrasas, hospitals, factories, conferences.
  • The card is mainly checked by looking at the photo at a gate or ward entrance.
  • Security staff need to read the role or category from a distance; a full-width colour band at the bottom of a portrait card is easy to spot.
  • Your logo is square or round, which sits naturally in a tall header.

Portrait cards hang straight because the slot sits in the middle of the short top edge, so the card's weight lies along the long axis. In our experience they also twist and flip less on a lanyard than landscape cards, especially with a double-sided design so that it does not matter which side faces out.

This is why so many school and garment-factory cards in Bangladesh are portrait: children and line workers wear them all day, and the person checking them looks at the face, the class or section, and the colour.

Official documents have moved the same way for the same reason. In 2023 ICAO, the body that sets travel document standards, published additional layout options for TD1-size cards that include a vertical format with a larger portrait. (Our topic is your organisation's own cards; government IDs follow their own rules.)

Horizontal ID cards: when landscape is the right choice

Choose landscape when most of these are true:

  • Cards are kept in wallets, phone cases, sleeves or clip holders and shown on request.
  • The card doubles as an access or proximity card that is tapped or swiped; most readers, sleeves and pre-printed RFID cards are designed around the horizontal card.
  • You need more text on the front: long names, a two-line organisation name, designation plus department.
  • Your logo is wide (a long wordmark), which fits a short, wide header.
  • You print in volume on A4 sheets and want the most cards per sheet.

Landscape is also the natural choice for membership cards, library cards and loyalty-style cards that live in a wallet next to bank cards. Corporate offices often prefer it because it looks like a business card and leaves room for a clean photo-left layout.

Lanyards, holders and slot punch position

Orientation decides the hardware, so decide it before you order holders.

Slot punch position. Most hand-held ID slot punches cut a slot of about 13 × 3 mm (½ × ⅛ in); some cut slightly longer slots (9/16 in). Centre it on the top edge, with its near side about 3–4 mm from the edge.

  • Portrait card: slot on the short (54 mm) edge, centred, so it is 27 mm from each side.
  • Landscape card that hangs: slot on the long (85.6 mm) edge, centred, 42.8 mm from each side.
  • Landscape card in a wallet or clip holder: no slot at all.

Keep the top 10 mm clear of text and the logo on any card that will be punched. If you do not know yet whether a card will be punched, design as if it will be.

Holders. Rigid and soft holders are sold as vertical or horizontal, each sized for a CR80 card. A portrait card in a horizontal holder hangs sideways, so order the holder that matches your design. If you buy holders and lanyards in bulk for a school or factory, buy them together with the first print run and test one card in one holder before ordering hundreds.

Lanyards for children. For schools and kindergartens, use breakaway lanyards that open under a sharp pull. Portrait cards suit them well because the card hangs centred.

How many cards per A4 sheet in each orientation?

On a portrait A4 sheet (210 × 297 mm) with small cutting gaps:

Orientation Cards per A4 sheet Grid Sheets for 300 cards (one side)
Landscape 10 2 columns × 5 rows 30
Landscape, wider margins 8 2 × 4 38
Portrait 9 3 × 3 34

Landscape saves about one sheet in ten. For most schools and offices that is a few sheets of card or PVC per year, so let the way the card is worn decide, not the paper count. If you print double-sided, the count is the same per side; the A4 ID card printing guide explains the cut marks, margins and how to line up the backs.

Tip: Never mix portrait and landscape cards on the same A4 sheet. It complicates the back-side alignment and the cutting, and one wrong cut ruins two cards.

Can you mix portrait and landscape in one organisation?

Yes, as long as it is a deliberate rule and not an accident. Within one card type, keep a single orientation: every staff card portrait, or every staff card landscape. Mixed orientations inside one group look careless and make holders and lanyards harder to stock.

Across card types, a different orientation can be useful:

  • Staff and students portrait, visitors landscape. A guard sees at a glance that a sideways card is a visitor pass. (See our visitor pass system guide for numbering and return rules.)
  • Employees portrait, contractors landscape, with a different band colour as well.
  • Event attendees on large portrait badges, crew on landscape CR80 cards clipped to a belt.

Always back the orientation signal with a printed word (VISITOR, CONTRACTOR, CREW). Orientation alone is easy to miss when a card is turned in a holder.

Decision table: portrait or landscape?

Organisation or use How the card is carried Recommended orientation Why
School, madrasa, kindergarten Lanyard, all day Portrait Photo first, hangs straight, colour band by class
Garment or other factory Lanyard or clip, all day Portrait Large worker number and section easy to read on the chest
Hospital or clinic Lanyard or badge reel Portrait Role band readable at a distance
Corporate office Wallet, sleeve or lanyard Either; landscape if it is also an access card Long designations fit; matches readers and sleeves
University Wallet and library desk Landscape Used like a bank card; more text fields
Conference or event Lanyard Portrait (often a larger badge) Name readable from a distance
Visitor pass Clip or lanyard, one day Landscape or the opposite of staff cards Instantly different from staff cards
Club or gym membership Wallet Landscape Lives next to other cards

Checklist before you lock the orientation

  1. Decide how the card is carried: lanyard, reel, clip, wallet or sleeve.
  2. Pick one orientation per card type and write it down for whoever prints next year.
  3. Set the design file to the right size: 54 × 85.6 mm (portrait) or 85.6 × 54 mm (landscape), 300 DPI, 3 mm bleed.
  4. Keep the top 10 mm clear if the card will be punched.
  5. Test the longest real name and the longest designation in the layout.
  6. Check the logo shape: square logos suit portrait headers, wide wordmarks suit landscape.
  7. Order holders that match the orientation (vertical or horizontal) and the slot position.
  8. Design the back in the same orientation as the front, so the card does not need turning to read.
  9. Print one test sheet, cut one card, punch it and hang it on a real lanyard before printing the batch.

Frequently asked questions

Is a vertical or horizontal ID card better?

Neither is better everywhere. Vertical (portrait) cards are better on lanyards and badge reels because they hang straight and show a larger photo. Horizontal (landscape) cards are better in wallets, sleeves and access-card readers, and they fit more text on the front.

What is the size of a portrait ID card?

A portrait ID card is the same CR80 card turned upright: 54 × 85.6 mm (2.13 × 3.37 in), or 638 × 1011 pixels at 300 DPI. With 3 mm bleed on every side, set up the file at 60 × 91.6 mm, about 709 × 1082 pixels.

Where should the hole be on a vertical ID card?

Centre the slot on the short top edge, 27 mm from each side, with its near edge about 3–4 mm from the top. Keep the top 10 mm of the design free of text and the logo so the punch does not cut into anything important.

Can I print portrait ID cards on a normal card printer?

Yes. Card printers and A4 printers handle both orientations, because the card itself is the same size. You only rotate the design; the printer driver or design app takes care of the rest. On A4 sheets, portrait cards fit 9 per sheet and landscape cards fit 10.

Should the back of the ID card have the same orientation as the front?

Usually yes. If the front is portrait and the back is landscape, people have to rotate the card to read the return address or terms, and back-side alignment on A4 sheets becomes harder. Keep both sides in the same orientation unless the back holds only a large QR code.

Why are most school ID cards vertical?

Because students wear them on lanyards all day, and the person checking them mainly needs to match the face to the photo. A portrait card hangs straight, gives the photo more room and leaves space for a full-width class or section colour band.
