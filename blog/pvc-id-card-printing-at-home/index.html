PVC ID Card Printing at Home: 4 Methods, Costs and Steps

PVC ID card printing without a card printer: inkjet PVC cards, A4 PVC sheet sets and laminated paper compared, with Bangladesh prices, cost per card and steps.

By Md. Ruhul Amin22 November 202613 min read

Key takeaways

  • You can make real plastic ID cards without a card printer in two ways: pre-cut inkjet PVC cards in a printer that has an ID card tray, or A4 inkjet PVC sheet sets ("dragon sheets") that you print, heat-laminate and die-cut.
  • A standard PVC card is 85.6 × 54 mm and 0.76 mm thick. A typical A4 sheet set builds that thickness from two 0.15 mm printable films around a 0.46 mm core.
  • In Bangladesh (listings checked November 2026), blank inkjet PVC cards cost about ৳10 each in 230-card boxes, plain card-printer blanks ৳4–8, and an A4 PVC sheet listing ৳2,500–2,800.
  • Inkjet PVC needs inkjet ink and patience: Epson asks you to let printed PVC cards dry for at least 24 hours. Never feed PVC into a laser printer.
  • For under a few hundred cards a year, laminated paper or A4 PVC sheets are usually the cheapest; a dedicated card printer only pays off at high volume.

Most people searching for PVC ID card printing want one thing: a card that feels like plastic, not paper, without buying a machine that costs as much as a motorbike. The good news is that you can get close, or all the way there, with an ordinary inkjet printer and a few supplies.

This guide compares the four realistic methods, shows what each one costs per card with prices we checked this week, and gives the exact steps for the two true-PVC methods you can do at home or in a small office.

What "PVC ID card" actually means

A PVC ID card is a rigid plastic card made of polyvinyl chloride in the CR80 format, the ID-1 size defined in ISO/IEC 7810: 85.6 × 53.98 mm with rounded corners of about 3.18 mm. The usual thickness is 0.76 mm, which card suppliers call "30 mil". (All the sizes, including pixels at 300 DPI, are in our ID card size chart.)

Plain PVC is smooth and non-absorbent, so inkjet ink just sits on top and smears. That is why there are two kinds of blank cards:

  • Card-printer blanks (Zebra, Evolis and similar). Uncoated PVC made for dye-sublimation card printers that use a ribbon. They will not take inkjet ink.
  • Inkjet PVC cards and sheets. PVC with a special ink-receptive coating, made for inkjet printers. These are what you need for home printing.

If a shop sells you "PVC cards" without saying which kind, ask. Buying the wrong blanks is the most common first mistake.

Method 1: Pre-cut inkjet PVC cards in a card tray

Some inkjet photo printers come with a tray that holds discs and PVC ID cards and feeds them flat through the printer. The Epson EcoTank L8050 is a widely available model in Bangladesh for this, and its user guide covers the process. Card printing is driver-controlled, so you cannot just push a card through a normal paper tray.

Blank inkjet PVC cards sold locally are CR80 size; one 230-card box on Othoba is listed as 600 microns thick and printable on both sides. Check the thickness against what your tray accepts before you buy a box.

Steps

  1. Design at exact card size. Export each card as a 300 DPI image or PDF at 85.6 × 54 mm. In CardCraft you can export a single card as a 300 DPI PNG, which is the easiest file to place on a card template.
  2. Use the right software and settings. Epson recommends its free Epson Photo+ app. If you print from other software, set the paper source to the Disc/ID card tray and the paper type to "PVC ID Card" (with or without borders).
  3. Send the job first, then load. Place the card printable side up in the tray slot, and insert the tray only when the printer asks for it. Inserting it early causes an error.
  4. Print one test card. Epson notes that smearing depends on the card brand, and that print density is set lower than for paper on purpose. If the surface stays tacky, reduce density.
  5. Let it dry. Epson's handling precautions say to dry printed cards for at least 24 hours, away from direct sunlight, and not to stack them until dry.
  6. Print the back. For double-sided cards, follow the on-screen prompt and run the tray again with the card flipped. Let the front dry first so you do not mark it while handling.
  7. Remove the tray after use. A tray left in the printer can hit the print head when the printer powers on or cleans itself.

Pros: no cutting, clean edges and corners straight from the box. Cons: slow (you feed the tray by hand), you need a specific printer, and inkjet ink on the surface is less scratch-resistant than a laminated card.

Method 2: A4 inkjet PVC sheet sets ("dragon sheets")

This is how many Bangladeshi and Indian print shops make school and office cards. You print a full A4 sheet of cards on any ordinary inkjet, fuse it into a solid plastic sheet with heat, and punch the cards out with a CR80 die cutter.

A set usually contains two printable films (front and back) and one plain core sheet. One listing on BDStall gives the layers as 0.15 mm films and a 0.46 mm core, laminated at 100–130 °C. Add them up and you get exactly the standard card:

An A4 sheet holds about 10 CR80 cards, the same 2 × 5 grid we use for paper printing. Indian marketplace listings sell 50-set packs (about 500 cards) for roughly ₹1,100–1,650, which works out to a few rupees per card before ink. The Bangladesh listings we found (৳2,500–2,800) do not state the pack size clearly, so ask the seller how many sets you get before comparing.

Steps

  1. Lay out the A4 sheet to match your die cutter. The cards must land exactly where the cutter punches. Most kits and cutters expect a 2 × 5 landscape grid; measure your cutter or ask the seller for its template.
  2. Print the front film and the back film. Use a normal inkjet, A4, 100% scale, borderless off, best quality. Kits differ on which side of the film is printed and whether the image must be mirrored, so follow the kit's sheet and test one set first.
  3. Dry the films completely. Trapped moisture causes bubbles and cloudy patches when the stack is heated.
  4. Stack and align. Front film, core, back film, in the order and orientation the kit specifies. Tape one edge so the layers cannot slide.
  5. Laminate. Feed the stack through a laminator or fusing press at the temperature the kit gives (100–130 °C in the listing above). A 0.76 mm stack is much thicker than a normal pouch, so check your laminator's maximum thickness; shops often use a press-type fusing machine for this step.
  6. Let it cool flat, under a book if needed, so the sheet does not curl.
  7. Punch the cards with a CR80 die cutter, one row at a time. Most CR80 die cutters round the corners in the same stroke.

Tip: The back sheet must line up with the front through the core. Print a test set on plain paper, hold it up to a window and check that the front and back outlines sit on top of each other before you use a real set.

Method 3: Laminated paper and photo paper

This is not PVC, but it is what most people mean when they say "plastic ID card" at a local shop, and for many uses it is good enough. Print the cards on glossy photo paper or 200–300 gsm card, laminate in pouches, cut with a trimmer and round the corners.

It costs the least, works on any printer, and with a thick pouch the card is stiff enough for a lanyard. It will not feel like a bank card, and the edges can peel if you cut too close to the paper. The full workflow, with layout and cutting tips, is in our A4 printing guide.

Method 4: Dedicated card printers

A dye-sublimation card printer prints directly onto plain PVC blanks with a ribbon, card by card, and many add a clear protective overlay. They are fast and consistent, and they can print both sides automatically on duplex models.

The catch is cost. The printer is the most expensive option here, and each ribbon has a fixed number of prints, so the running cost depends on the ribbon you buy (we have not measured local ribbon prices). If you print a few hundred cards once a year for a school, the printer will sit idle for eleven months. We will cover the break-even math between a card printer, A4 printing and a print shop in a separate guide.

Which method should you choose?

Use this quick decision rule:

Your situation Go with
One-day event, visitor or conference passes Laminated paper
Fewer than 100 durable cards a year, want no cutting Pre-cut inkjet PVC in a tray printer
100–1,000 cards a year, school or office batches A4 inkjet PVC sheet sets
Thousands of cards a year, daily reprints, chip or stripe cards Dedicated card printer
No printer and no time Send a print-ready PDF to a print shop

If you send the work to a shop, a clean print-ready file saves money and avoids mistakes. CardCraft exports A4 sheets with cut marks as a 300 DPI PDF, plus single-card PNG images, so you can hand over either format.

Checklist before you print a PVC batch

  • Design is exactly 85.6 × 54 mm, or 1011 × 638 px at 300 DPI.
  • Names, photos and ID numbers sit at least 3 mm inside the edge (the die cutter or tray can drift by a millimetre or two).
  • You bought inkjet-coated PVC, not card-printer blanks.
  • Printer is an inkjet. Never put PVC cards or sheets into a laser printer; the fuser is hot enough to warp them.
  • Print scale is 100%, borderless off, quality set to best.
  • One test card or test sheet printed, dried and checked for smears.
  • Front and back alignment checked against a window or light.
  • Drying space ready: cards laid out flat, not stacked, for the drying time the maker gives (24 hours on Epson's guidance).
  • The back of the card carries your return address and validity. If you are unsure what to write, use our ready-to-copy back side text.
  • Spare blanks for reprints (5–10% of the batch is a sensible buffer).

Frequently asked questions

Can I print PVC ID cards with a normal inkjet printer?

Yes, in two ways. Use A4 inkjet PVC sheet sets, which run through any inkjet like paper and are then laminated and die-cut, or use pre-cut inkjet PVC cards in a printer that has a disc/ID card tray. Plain uncoated PVC cards will smear in any inkjet.

Can a laser printer print on PVC cards?

No. A laser printer fuses toner with heat, and PVC can warp or melt inside the fuser, which can damage the printer. Inkjet PVC products are made for inkjet printers only; for laser printers, use paper or card stock and laminate it.

What is the price of a PVC ID card in Bangladesh?

Blank inkjet PVC cards are listed at about ৳10 each in 230-card boxes, and plain card-printer blanks at ৳4–8 each (November 2026 listings). Ink, lamination and labour are extra, so a finished card from a shop costs more; ask for a quote per hundred cards.

What is a dragon sheet for ID cards?

It is the trade name for an A4 inkjet PVC sheet set: two printable films and a plastic core that you print, stack and heat-fuse into a single card-thick sheet. You then punch out about 10 CR80 cards per sheet with a die cutter.

How long does ink take to dry on a PVC card?

It depends on the card coating and the ink. Epson's guidance for its PVC card tray is to let printed cards dry for at least 24 hours, away from direct sunlight, before stacking or using them. Some card brands claim faster drying, but test before you stack a batch.

Is a PVC ID card better than a laminated paper card?

PVC is more rigid, more water-resistant and lasts longer in a wallet or holder, so it suits staff and student cards used all year. Laminated paper is cheaper and faster for short-term cards such as event and visitor passes.
